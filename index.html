Yogendra Limbu

Biography

I am Yogendra Limbu from Nepal. I earned my Master’s degree in Physics from Tribhuvan University, Nepal, where my thesis focused on the First Principles Study of Properties of Functionalized MXene (Ti2N) and Defect on MXene Mono-Layers. In August 2022, I joined the research group of Prof. Michael E. Flatté at The University of Iowa to pursue a Ph.D. in Physics. My doctoral research centered on the ab initio investigation of strongly correlated quantum materials, with particular emphasis on transition-metal- and rare-earth-based systems embedded in wide-band-gap semiconductors and insulators, as well as rare-earth-containing molecular complexes. My dissertation, titled "Ab initio Study of Strongly Correlated Systems: Rare Earth Hosted Oxides and Molecule and Two Dimensional MXene", explored the structural, electronic, magnetic, and excitation properties of these materials using first-principles computational methods. I received my Ph.D. in Physics from The University of Iowa in April 2026.

Strongly Correlated Quantum Materials

Strongly correlated quantum materials, such as rare-earth and transition-metal compounds, exhibit emergent electronic and magnetic phenomena that arise from strong electron–electron interactions. These systems play a central role in quantum information science and spintronics. Understanding their functional properties requires detailed knowledge of their electronic structure, magnetic ordering, and the many-body effects associated with localized 3d and 4f electrons.

Research Motivation

Rare-earth ions embedded in solid-state hosts are promising platforms for quantum technologies due to their partially filled 4f electronic shells, which are well shielded by outer orbitals. This shielding leads to narrow optical transitions, long spin coherence times, and weak coupling to the environment—key properties for applications such as quantum memories, quantum networks, and microwave-to-optical quantum transduction.

In many rare-earth systems, the spin and orbital states can be optically addressed and coherently manipulated, enabling their use as scalable solid-state qubits. A critical aspect of these systems is the crystal-field interaction, which lifts the degeneracy of the 4f electronic levels and strongly influences the energy-level structure and optical response.

In addition, rare-earth dopants can significantly modify the electronic and magnetic properties of host materials through multiple stable charge states and localized defect states. A quantitative understanding of these effects requires accurate modeling of defect formation energies, charge-state stability, and the interplay between local chemical environment and electronic structure.

My research focuses on systematically investigating these phenomena using first-principles methods, with emphasis on electronic structure, magnetic properties, and defect physics in rare-earth-doped systems. In particular, I aim to understand how local coordination environments modulate crystal-field energy levels and thereby influence the performance of rare-earth ions in quantum information science applications.
